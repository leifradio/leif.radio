CW is both faster and clearer when you do not have to spell out every sentence. That is why we use Q-codes and procedural signals, known as prosigns. A few characters are enough to ask a question, give an answer or steer the QSO itself, and the other station knows exactly what you mean.

## Q-codes

Q-codes come from international maritime and commercial radio. They are three-letter abbreviations beginning with Q, and they are still used a great deal on CW, precisely to keep QSOs short.

The same code can be either a question or a statement. With a question mark after it, for example `QTH?`, it is a question: "What is your location?" Without a question mark, `QTH BORLANGE`, it is an answer or a statement: "My location is Borlänge."

Here are the Q-codes you will meet most often in ordinary amateur radio CW QSOs.

| Code | Question (?) | Answer or statement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| QRG | What is my exact frequency? | Your exact frequency is … kHz. |
| QRL | Are you busy? Is this frequency in use? | I am busy. This frequency is in use. |
| QRM | Is my transmission being interfered with? | Your transmission is being interfered with (by other stations or electrical noise). |
| QRN | Are you troubled by atmospheric noise? | I am troubled by atmospheric noise, for example thunderstorms. |
| QRO | Shall I increase power? | Increase power. I am running high power. |
| QRP | Shall I decrease power? | Decrease power. I am running low power, usually no more than 5 W. |
| QRQ | Shall I send faster? | Send faster. |
| QRS | Shall I send more slowly? | Send more slowly. |
| QRT | Shall I stop sending? | Stop sending. I am closing down (going QRT). |
| QRU | Have you anything for me? | I have nothing for you. |
| QRV | Are you ready? | I am ready. |
| QRX | Shall I stand by? When will you call me again? | Stand by. I will call you again at … (`QRX 5` = wait five minutes). |
| QRZ | Who is calling me? | You are being called by … |
| QSB | Are my signals fading? | Your signals are fading (going up and down in strength). |
| QSD | Is my keying defective? | Your keying is defective or uneven. |
| QSK | Can you hear me between your signals? Can you use break-in? | I can hear you between my signals. I am using break-in (QSK). |
| QSL | Can you acknowledge receipt? Do you confirm? | I acknowledge receipt. I confirm. Also used for a QSL card or other confirmation of the QSO. |
| QSO | Can you communicate with …? | I can communicate with … A QSO is a radio contact. |
| QSP | Will you relay to …? | I will relay to … |
| QST | (rarely used as a question) | General call to all stations. A message to all radio amateurs. |
| QSX | Will you listen for … on … kHz? | I am listening for … on … kHz. |
| QSY | Shall I change to another frequency? | Change to another frequency (or to … kHz). I am changing frequency. |
| QTH | What is your location? | My location is … |
| QTR | What is the correct time? | The correct time is … |
| QTC | How many messages do you have? | I have … messages for you. |

A few codes are especially useful to know well. Send `QRL?` on a frequency before calling CQ, so that you do not walk into an ongoing QSO. It is perfectly all right to ask for `QRS` – it is better to slow down than to miss what the other station is saying. `QSL` and `QSO` have also become ordinary words among radio amateurs: a QSO is a contact, and a QSL is a confirmation, for example a card or a LoTW log entry.

## Prosigns

Prosigns, or procedural signals, are used to structure a CW QSO: when a message ends, when you turn it over to the other station, when the contact is finished, and so on.

Most prosigns are two letters sent as one character, with no space between the letters. They are often written with a bar over both letters, or in angle brackets, for example `<AR>` or `<SK>`. They are not two characters you spell one after the other, but a Morse signal of their own.

Two common exceptions: `K` is simply the letter K, and `CQ` is usually sent as two ordinary letters, C and Q, with a normal letter space. Both still belong among the procedural signals you use all the time.

| Sign | Morse | Meaning and when it is used |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `<AR>` | `· − · − ·` | End of message. Sent when the content is finished, often just before the call signs and `K` or `<SK>`. |
| `<AS>` | `· − · · ·` | Wait, stand by for a moment. |
| `<BK>` | `− · · · − · −` | Break. Used to turn it over to the other station at once, or to break into a QSO. |
| `<BT>` | `− · · · −` | A separator between parts of a transmission, rather like a new paragraph. For example between the signal report, name and QTH. |
| `<CL>` | `− · − · · − · ·` | The station is closing down and going off the air. Often sent after `<SK>`: first the QSO ends, then the station shuts down. |
| `<CQ>` | `− · − ·`  `− − · −` | A general call to any station. Usually sent as two ordinary letters, C and Q, with a normal letter space. |
| `<HH>` (eight dits) | `· · · · · · · ·` | Error. You have sent a mistake, and then you repeat the word, corrected. |
| `K` | `− · −` | Go ahead. The other station – or anyone, for example after CQ – may transmit. |
| `<KN>` | `− · − − ·` | Go ahead, but only the station that has been called. Other stations are asked to stand by. |
| `<SK>` / `<VA>` | `· · · − · −` | End of contact. The QSO is finished. `<SK>` and `<VA>` are the same Morse signal. |
| `<SN>` / `<VE>` | `· · · − ·` | Understood. `<SN>` and `<VE>` are the same Morse signal. |
| `<SOS>` | `· · · − − − · · ·` | The international distress signal. Sent as one continuous signal, with no spaces between S, O and S. To be used only in a genuine emergency. |

In the example on the [CW QSO](/en/cw/cw-qso/) page you will see `<AR>`, `K` and `<SK>` in exactly the places where you turn the contact over or bring it to an end.
