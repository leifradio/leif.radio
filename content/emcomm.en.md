For me, emergency communications means being able to communicate even when the usual channels are not working. We are used to being able to make calls, send messages and look up information at any time. But during prolonged power outages or disruptions to mobile networks and the internet, for example, those options may become limited.

Amateur radio can then provide an alternative. With a radio, a suitable antenna and an independent power supply, it is possible to establish radio links without relying on the local mobile network. That does not mean contact will always be possible. Range is affected by factors including frequency, antenna, terrain and propagation – and there must be someone listening at the other end.

## Knowledge that needs to be put into practice

A radio in a box does not make you prepared. I think the important thing is to use the equipment, understand its limitations and practise under different conditions. Setting up an antenna, choosing a suitable frequency and getting a clear message across are skills that need to be kept alive.

There is a natural connection here to my interest in portable amateur radio. When I take my radio out into nature, I get to practise setting up a working station with the equipment and power I have with me. That is enjoyable in itself, but it also provides experience that can be valuable when conditions are not as comfortable as at home.

## Winlink and VarAC

My interest in emergency communications mainly involves Winlink and VarAC, which I use with the callsign SM4T.

Winlink makes it possible to send and receive email-like messages over radio. My own station does not need an internet connection, but reaching ordinary email normally involves a radio gateway that is itself connected to the internet. Direct transfers between radio stations that support this are also possible.

I use VarAC for text-based communication and messaging over radio. It allows written information to be exchanged directly between stations, which can be useful when names, locations or other details need to come across clearly.

Both require working equipment, the right settings and suitable radio conditions. That is why I see regular use and practice as an important part of preparedness.

## A complement, not a guarantee

Amateur radio does not replace society’s normal communications systems and is no guarantee of being able to reach help. For me, it is about having another way to receive and send information, and knowing when and how it can be used.

That is also what makes emergency communications interesting: the combination of technology, practical skills and the ability to make contact when conditions change.
